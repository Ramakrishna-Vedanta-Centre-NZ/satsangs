Free Will and Divine Will: Interactive Companion

Six small experiments to accompany the article Free Will and Divine Will: Am I the Doer? Try each one before reading its part of the article. These ideas are easier to feel than to read about.

1. The stroboscope: reality unchanged, appearance changed

The fan really turns 5 times a second (ff). Start with the strobe at 0 (off): appearance and reality turn together. Then slide the strobe frequency (fs) up towards the fan’s, through it and beyond, and watch the red blade: the apparent motion is Δf = ff − fs. Both drawings run in the same slow motion, so their speeds can be compared directly. (For simplicity the drawing follows Δf all the way; a real strobe set far below the fan’s speed shows more complicated patterns.)

3. The dial of the mind

A contemplative model for teaching, not a scientific measurement. The three spaces are not separate zones but one continuum, from the grossest to the finest. In every case, the dashed arrow traces the impulse from the Source (6), where all action begins, down to the setting where it is lived. The ordinary-waking setting draws on research; the others are teaching placements.

4. Two wills: from struggle to coherence

My will is not a rival standing outside the Divine Will. It lives inside it, small and off-centre: limited in reach, and tilted towards “me and mine”. As surrender grows, it widens and comes to centre, bridging the gap, until the two are one. Move the slider.

5. The dial of experience: Δf and Δφ together

Every experience is a meeting of two waves in the mind (Part 4 of the article). Turn the two dials and watch the same object become a different experience. Δf is the gap between the mind's rhythm and its object: it is felt as time. Δφ is the tilt of the I-maker: it is felt as “I am the doer”. Saṁskāra, body and noise are held steady here.

The Witness: unchanged. Whatever the dials show, this does not move. Every setting is known by the same Seer, who is not one of the variables. “Then the Seer abides in its own nature” (Yoga Sūtra 1.3).

6. Libet’s experiment: watching an action unfold

Drag the time slider, or press Play, and watch one simple action unfold: a flick of the wrist. Each moment is shown in two lights: what the instruments record, and the contemplative reading of the four levels of Vāk (Part 6 of the article). Turn on the veto to see an impulse that takes form but is not carried out.

Timings follow Libet and colleagues (1983): the readiness potential begins about 0.55 s before the movement, and the urge is felt about 0.2 s before it. The veto follows Libet (1985); later work found a movement can be cancelled only up to about 0.2 s before it begins (Schultze-Kraft and colleagues, 2016). The dot on Libet’s clock goes round once every 2.56 s; “W” marks where it was when the urge was felt. The Vāk reading is a contemplative interpretation offered as a bridge, not a scientific result.

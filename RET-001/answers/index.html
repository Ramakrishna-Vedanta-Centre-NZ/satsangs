QUESTION 1 · ANALYSIS

Two members each give four hours a month to their branch. After three years one has grown calmer, kinder and less easily hurt; the other is tired and quietly resentful. What does the teaching on yajña see as the deciding difference?

A. The inner attitude: one offers the work and lets go of “I and mine”, the other works for return or from pressure.✓ Correct answer
B. The kind of work each was given, since some tasks are naturally far more uplifting to do than others.
C. Their natural temperament, because service can only bring out more clearly what was already in a person.
D. The thanks each one received, since warm appreciation from others is what keeps any volunteer growing over the years.

The Gītā says that work done for any purpose other than yajña binds us, while work offered as yajña sets us free (3.9). The same four hours can bind one person and purify another, because the difference lies in the attitude, not in the hours. The other options hold some truth: tasks differ, temperaments differ, thanks is pleasant. But none of them decides whether service becomes growth. Only the spirit of offering does that, and it is in our own hands. See slide 9.

Reflect: After my last piece of service, did I feel lighter or heavier, and what was I holding on to?

QUESTION 2 · EVALUATION

Four members explain why they joined NZICA. Which one has most fully understood the motto ātmano mokṣārthaṁ jagad-hitāya ca, “for one’s own liberation and for the welfare of the world”?

A. “I come to serve the community. My spiritual life is a separate, private matter that I keep at home.”
B. “I come mainly for my own peace of mind. Helping others is a pleasant extra that comes along with it.”
C. “First I must become pure and wise myself. Only once I have reached that point will I begin serving others.”
D. “Serving with the right attitude purifies my mind, and a purer mind serves better; each helps the other.”✓ Correct answer

The small word ca, “and”, joins the two aims into one path. To grow means to expand from the small self to the larger Self; to serve is to acknowledge our oneness with all. Each of the other three members has kept one half and lost the other: service without inner growth, growth without real service, or growth postponed until some future day. Swami Vivekananda’s ideal is that we grow through serving and serve better as we grow. See slide 9.

Reflect: Where in my life do I keep “my spiritual practice” and “my service” in separate boxes?

QUESTION 3 · EVALUATION

After a year of regular seva, how could a member best tell whether it has become spiritual growth and not just more activity?

A. By the number of hours worked, events organised and people served, all carefully recorded over the year.
B. By the appreciation and recognition received from the community and from the leaders of the association.
C. By Swami Ranganathananda’s test: more oneness with others, more inner peace, and that peace radiating around, even in hard moments.✓ Correct answer
D. By a growing certainty that one’s own way of serving is the right one, and that others should follow it.

Swami Ranganathananda asked: Are you growing spiritually? Can you feel oneness with others? Have you peace within yourself? And do you radiate it around you? These are signs of character, not of activity. Hours and events can grow while the ego grows with them; recognition is a return, not a sign of growth; and certainty that others should follow our way is often ego in a new form. See slide 10.

Reflect: Over the past year, has my peace grown, and do the people around me feel it?

QUESTION 4 · ANALYSIS

Arjun says: “I have worked hard for everything I have. I owe nothing to anyone.” His hard work is real. What does the wheel of yajña (Gītā 3.14 to 3.16) show that his view leaves out?

A. That effort counts for very little in the end, because every result is already decided by fate and the devas.
B. That he should give generously now, so that good fortune and success keep flowing back to him in all the years ahead.
C. That his effort rests on countless gifts he never earned, so giving back completes the circle rather than being charity.✓ Correct answer
D. That he owes a fixed debt to society which he can repay in full once and then consider closed for good.

The Gītā describes life as a wheel: beings live on food, food comes from rain, rain from yajña, and yajña from action (3.14). Arjun’s effort stands on the gifts of parents, teachers, the land, the rain and the community. Seeing this does not cancel his effort; it places it inside a larger circle of giving. The wrong options turn the teaching into fatalism, into a business deal, or into a debt that can be paid off and forgotten. Gratitude is not a bill; it is a way of living. See slide 14.

Reflect: List five things I received this week without paying for them. What would giving back look like?

QUESTION 5 · EVALUATION

After reading that one who enjoys life’s gifts without giving is “a thief” (3.12), Maya becomes anxious and guilty, and gives out of fear that she is never doing enough. Which advice would help her most?

A. She should work out roughly what she has received over the years and give back that amount, so the account is settled.
B. The word is meant to awaken gratitude, not fear. Giving that flows from gratitude frees the mind; giving from guilt keeps it anxious.✓ Correct answer
C. Her anxiety shows she is taking the verse seriously, so she should keep increasing her giving until the guilt goes away.
D. She should set the verse aside, since a teaching first given for ancient fire rituals does not really apply to modern life.

Strong words in scripture are meant to wake us, not to frighten us. When giving comes from fear or guilt, the mind stays centred on itself: “Am I good enough?” When it comes from gratitude, the mind turns outward and becomes light. Exact accounting misses the spirit, more giving will not cure guilt, and setting the verse aside throws away a teaching that still applies to every one of us. See slide 15.

Reflect: Does my giving come more from gratitude or from a sense of obligation? How does each one feel inside?

QUESTION 6 · APPLICATION

A busy family rarely eats together, and the teenagers find religious customs dull. Which first step would best bring the spirit of Gītā 3.13, sharing food as prasāda, into this home?

A. Print verse 3.13 in Sanskrit and put it on the fridge, so that everyone in the family sees it every single day.
B. Cook more elaborate meals at the weekend, so that the family learns to value food and the time spent eating it.
C. Let each person say a private prayer before eating, whenever they eat, so that no one feels the practice is forced.
D. Once a week, pause together before a shared meal to remember where the food came from, offer it, then share it.✓ Correct answer

Verse 3.13 says that those who eat what remains after offering are freed from faults, while those who cook only for themselves eat sin. The point is a change of attitude: food becomes a gift received and shared. A small, regular, shared pause does this best, and it can grow. A verse on the fridge informs but does not transform. Better food may please without changing the attitude. Private prayer is good, but the verse is about sharing. See slide 15.

Reflect: Can I pause before my next meal to remember the many hands that brought it to me?

QUESTION 7 · ANALYSIS

At a food drive Raj thinks: “I am the one helping these poor people.” He works hard and means well. What is the hidden error in his feeling, and what corrects it at the root?

A. He is helping too few people; reaching many more would gradually turn his pride into real and lasting compassion.
B. He uses the word “poor”; if he simply chose kinder words, the problem in his attitude would disappear by itself.
C. He sees himself as the high giver and them as low receivers; seeing all as one Reality (4.24) turns pride into gratitude.✓ Correct answer
D. He is enjoying the work too much; real service should feel like a hard duty, carried out without taking any pleasure in it at all.

Brahmārpaṇam (4.24): the offering, the fire, the one who offers and the act of offering are all Brahman. Raj’s error is the line he draws between himself and those he serves. Numbers, words and feelings of enjoyment are all on the surface. When he sees the Divine in each person, pride gives way to reverence, and, as Swami Vivekananda taught, he will feel grateful to them for the chance to serve. See slide 16.

QUESTION 8 · EVALUATION

Mohan says: “Swami Vivekananda called image worship only preliminary, so I have stopped my shrine worship and use that time to volunteer.” How should we evaluate his decision?

A. He is right, because real worship is service, and time at the shrine is time taken away from people in need.
B. He has misread the teaching: a beginning is meant to grow, not to be discarded. Worship and service nourish each other.✓ Correct answer
C. He is right only if he is a monk; householders should keep worship at home and leave service to the Mission.
D. He is wrong, because service is only a preparation, and the real worship happens before the sacred image in the family shrine.

“He who sees Shiva in the poor, in the weak, and in the diseased, really worships Shiva; and if he sees Shiva only in the image, his worship is but preliminary.” Preliminary means a first step, not a mistake. The love and remembrance practised at the shrine are what let us see the same Divine in people. As Swami Ranganathananda put it, spiritual growth comes from meditation inwardly and service outwardly. Mohan has cut one of the two wings. See slide 17.

QUESTION 9 · SYNTHESIS

A coordinator notices that some helpers at a community pantry speak down to the guests. Bringing together “the giver is blessed” and serving the living God in people, which change would address this at the root?

A. Help the helpers see each visit as a privilege: greet and thank every guest, serve with respect, and remember who is blessed.✓ Correct answer
B. Make a firm rule that helpers say no more to the guests than is needed, so that hurtful remarks are simply prevented.
C. Award helpers a badge for every month of service, so that they feel valued themselves and become more generous.
D. Move the service to home delivery, so that guests and helpers no longer meet and there is no chance of disrespect.

Swami Vivekananda said: “It is not the receiver that is blessed, but it is the giver.” He called it a great privilege to be allowed to worship God by helping our fellow-man. The other changes manage the symptom: silence, rewards and distance do not change how helpers see the guests. Only a change of vision, learned together and practised at every visit, removes the root. See slide 18.

Reflect: When I last helped someone, did I feel that I was doing them a favour, or that they were doing me one?

Read each person’s inner attitude. Which step on the ladder of purification best describes where each one stands now? Choose one for each line.

1. Arun gladly takes on a committee role, but feels let down when his name is left off the thank-you list.✓ Giving for a return
2. Meena gives quietly every week and is untroubled whether anyone notices, because giving is simply right.✓ Giving because it is right

Arun is giving for a return, here recognition (rājasika, 17.12); notice that the sign is not the act but the disappointment. Meena gives because it is right, seeking nothing (sāttvika, 17.11). Each step loosens “I and mine” a little more, and the same person may stand on different steps on different days. See slide 19.

QUESTION 11 · APPLICATION

Anita is a retired teacher who lives alone on a small pension. She says: “I have no money, so I cannot really do seva.” Which plan best fits the teaching on the many forms of yajña?

A. Save a little each week until she can make one sizeable donation, since material giving is the clearest form of seva.
B. Accept that her years of seva have passed, and support those who still can with her good wishes and her thanks.
C. Offer what she has: help children of new families with reading, and pray for others daily. Both are yajña.✓ Correct answer
D. Join the busiest committee she can find, since only public, organised work really counts as service to the community.

The Gītā lists many offerings: of wealth, of discipline, of study and knowledge (4.25 to 4.30). Swami Vivekananda spoke of physical, intellectual and spiritual help, and called the gift of knowledge “a far higher gift than that of food and clothes”. Anita’s years as a teacher are a treasure to offer, and prayer for others is itself an offering. The other plans assume that only money or public work counts, or that age closes the door. See slide 20.

QUESTION 12 · EVALUATION

Ashok says: “Charity begins at home. I will care for my family now, and serve the community later, when the children have grown up.” In the light of the five daily offerings and the widening circles, how should we evaluate this?

A. He is right, because duty to the family must come first, and the wider circles can wait until that duty is done.
B. The family is the first field of seva, but the circles widen together. Small offerings to others can start now and teach the children.✓ Correct answer
C. He is wrong, because attachment to family is a form of “mine” and has to be given up for selfless public service.
D. Only the widest circle, service to all beings, counts as real yajña, since caring for one’s own is never selfless.

The pañca mahāyajña, the five daily offerings, widen our care step by step from the self to all beings, and every householder practises them every day, not one after another over a lifetime. Caring for children and elders is truly yajña. But when the wider circles are postponed, the children learn that care stops at the front door. Children learn seva by seeing it. Despising family duty, or calling it never selfless, misses that the home is the first circle. See slide 21.

QUESTION 13 · ANALYSIS

Vikram works hard at every event, but afterwards he complains about the organisers to anyone who will listen. In terms of yajña in thought, word and deed, where is his offering incomplete, and why does it matter for him?

A. In word and thought: his hands offer, but his speech and mind still grasp, so resentment undoes the purification.✓ Correct answer
B. In deed: if he did even more work at each event, there would be no time left for the complaints to arise.
C. Nowhere at all: what counts is the help actually given, and private complaints do not affect the service itself in any way.
D. In leadership: he should ask for a role on the committee, so that he can put right the things that annoy him.

Our spiritual tradition speaks of purity manasā, vācā, karmaṇā: in thought, word and deed. Vikram’s deeds are an offering, but his words and thoughts take back what his hands gave. The work could purify his mind, yet resentment keeps feeding “I and mine”. More work only adds fatigue, saying it does not matter ignores the effect on him and on others, and a committee role would give the same grasping a larger stage. See slide 22.

QUESTION 14 · MATCHING · ANALYSIS

Swami Vivekananda taught that real service needs heart, head and hands in harmony. In each case, what is missing? Choose one for each line.

1. Ravi designs an efficient roster for the meals service, but treats both volunteers and guests as names on a sheet.✓ The heart is missing
2. Shanti is moved to tears by a family’s hardship and rushes to give whatever comes to mind, though it is not what they need.✓ The head is missing

Ravi has head and hands but no heart, so his service becomes cold. Shanti has heart and hands but no head, so her help misses the real need. QUESTION 15 · EVALUATION

Kiran hears that we should be “merely an instrument” of the Divine and concludes: “Then the results are up to God, so it does not matter much how carefully I prepare.” How should we evaluate his reasoning?

A. He is right: an instrument has no responsibility of its own, so careful preparation would only feed the ego of the doer.
B. He is right about results but wrong about effort, since effort is what earns us a good result from the Lord in return.
C. He has misunderstood: being an instrument is a state reserved for saints, and ordinary members should simply work hard.
D. He has turned it upside down: an instrument must be well tuned. Give full skill and care, and let go only of “I am the doer” and anxiety.✓ Correct answer

Gītā 2.50 says yoga is skill in action, and Sri Ramakrishna prayed, “O Mother, Thou art the Operator and I am the machine.” A machine the Operator can use well is clean, oiled and in good order. Being an instrument removes the ego and the anxiety, not the care. The second option turns offering into a bargain; the third puts the ideal out of reach, though every sevak can begin to practise it in small things today. See slide 25.

QUESTION 16 · EVALUATION

Leela gives a large donation anonymously, but each month she checks the newsletter and feels a little let down that no one has guessed it was her. How would the Gītā’s teaching on sāttvika giving (17.20) assess this, and what is the way forward?

A. Fully sāttvika, because only the anonymous act itself counts, not the passing feelings that come up afterwards.
B. Of no spiritual value, since a gift mixed with any wish for credit is spoiled, so she should stop until her motive is pure.
C. Outwardly sāttvika, inwardly still seeking a return. Keep giving, notice the wish kindly and offer it too.✓ Correct answer
D. Tāmasika, because giving in secret lacks the honesty and openness of a gift that is made plainly in public.

The Gītā calls a gift sāttvika when it is given because it is right to give, to a worthy cause, at the right time, expecting nothing in return (17.20). Leela’s act meets this outwardly, but a quiet wish for credit remains. That is how purification usually works: the act comes first and the motive is refined by practice. Calling it already perfect stops her growth; calling it worthless would stop the giving that is purifying her. Secrecy is not tamas. See slide 26.

QUESTION 17 · ANALYSIS

Two branches keep competing to hold the bigger Diwali event, and members of each speak a little scornfully of the other. What is really happening, according to the teaching on “I and mine”?

A. This is healthy competition, which spurs both branches on to do more good, so nothing needs to change here.
B. The ego has not gone; it has widened to “my branch”. The cure is to see both as offerings to one fire.✓ Correct answer
C. There are simply too many events; if each branch held fewer, the rivalry between them would fade by itself.
D. The fault lies mainly with whichever branch began the rivalry, so it is up to that branch to make the first move.

Swami Vivekananda taught that the whole secret of unselfish work is giving up “I and mine”. Ego can hide inside good work: “my branch, my event, my success”. A wider “mine” is still “mine”. When both branches see their events as offerings into one sacred fire, each can rejoice in the other’s success. Fewer events, blame, or calling it healthy competition all leave the root untouched. See slide 27.

QUESTION 18 · SYNTHESIS

In a committee meeting Neha is criticised unfairly for a project she leads. Drawing on Gītā 12.13 (free from “mine” and ego, forgiving, even in pain and pleasure) and the idea that the work is the Lord’s, which response shows these qualities growing?

A. She listens calmly, corrects anything true, lets go of the hurt and bears no grudge: the work is not hers to defend.✓ Correct answer
B. She resigns quietly from the project, since a sevak should avoid all conflict and never stand in another’s way.
C. She defends herself firmly, to protect the good name of the project and of everyone who has worked hard on it.

Verse 12.13 describes one who “hates no being, is friendly and compassionate to all, free from ‘mine’ and from ego, even in pain and pleasure, and forgiving”. If the project is an offering, criticism does not have to wound the ego, and any truth in it can be used. Resigning, defending or quiet avoidance can each look wise, but each is still about protecting “I”. Forgiveness here is not weakness; it is freedom. See slide 28.

QUESTION 21 · APPLICATION

At his evening review Rohan notices that he was impatient twice during the day. In the spirit of the sevak’s evening offering, what should he do with this?

A. Make up for it by fasting or doing extra service the next day, so that the fault is properly paid for.
B. Note it honestly, offer it with the day’s work to the Lord, resolve gently to do better, and rest without self-condemnation.✓ Correct answer
C. Think only of what went well, since dwelling on faults weakens confidence and the will to serve others.

The evening offering asks two gentle questions: Where did I serve? Where did “I and mine” creep in? Then everything, success and failure alike, is offered to the Lord. Dev gave years to his branch committee. When he is not re-elected he feels he has lost himself, and he stops coming. In the light of “Who are we?”, what has really happened?

A. He took a role for his identity; the “unripe I” of position was hurt. His true Self is untouched, and he can still serve as a member.✓ Correct answer

QUESTION 23 · SYNTHESIS

A branch is planning Diwali. Some elders want every detail done exactly as in their home town; several young members find parts of it meaningless and are drifting away. Bringing together the test for what to protect and what to let go, and “each took up their own share”, what is the wisest way forward?

A. Do everything exactly as before, since our heritage is precious and changing any part of it means losing it for good.

Freezing every detail, discarding the heritage, or splitting the community all fail the test of unity. See slide 36.

QUESTION 24 · SYNTHESIS

NZICA wants its branches to work together on one national project. Which approach best embodies the Saṁjñāna Sūkta, “walk together, speak together, let your minds be of one accord”, and the image of many offerings into one fire?

A. The national office designs every detail, and every branch carries out the same tasks, so that unity is ensured.

QUESTION 25 · SYNTHESIS

Six months after the retreat, Priya, who heard the talk, read the paper and scored well on the quiz, notices that nothing in her daily life has changed. Which next step best reflects śravaṇa, manana, nididhyāsana and Swami Vivekananda’s idea of assimilation?

A. Read the study paper again and retake the quiz until she gets full marks, so that the ideas are firmly in place.
B. Attend more talks on other topics, so that a wider range of knowledge gradually leads to some change in her life.
C. Accept that this path does not suit her nature, and look for a different spiritual practice that will work better.
D. Choose one idea, such as offering each morning’s work, live it daily until it is natural, then take up another.✓ Correct answer

Swami Vivekananda said: “If you have assimilated five ideas and made them your life and character, you have more education than any man who has got by heart a whole library.” Śravaṇa is hearing, manana is reflection, and nididhyāsana is living the idea until it becomes part of us. Priya has done the first two. More reading, more talks or a new path all stay at the level of information; one idea lived daily is what changes character. See slide 38.

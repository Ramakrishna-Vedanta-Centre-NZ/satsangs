QUESTION 1

A friend says: “I am not holy enough to approach the Divine. I would feel like a fraud in front of the Mother.” How does the satsang’s picture of the Mother answer this?

A. She is the Power behind the universe and far too vast to be approached in a personal way by an ordinary person.
B. A child needs no qualification to run to its mother: the Mother is approachable, and he may simply come.✓ Correct answer
C. She is approached through the image in the Durgā Pūjā alone, and nowhere else, so he must wait until the festival.
D. She receives only those who have first purified themselves through years of discipline, so he should keep preparing.

In the Indian spiritual tradition She is the one who gives birth to the universe, nourishes and protects it, and finally draws it back into Herself. Above all a mother is approachable: a child needs no qualification to run to Her. The image in the Durgā Pūjā is a doorway and not a limit, and the same Mother lives in the universe and in us. See slide 4.

QUESTION 2

A student says: “Advaita worships Brahman, and Śākta worship adores a Mother. These must be two different realities.” How would Sri Ramakrishna answer?

A. They are one Reality, but the Mother is only a lower, symbolic form that is given up when Brahman is known.
B. They are one Reality: Brahman at rest and Śakti in action, as inseparable as fire and its power to burn.✓ Correct answer
C. They are two realities, and only the worshipper of the Mother reaches the higher one in the end.
D. They are two realities that cooperate, one inactive and one active, much as two partners share a task.

Sri Ramakrishna said that when he thought of the Supreme as inactive, neither creating, preserving nor destroying, he called it Brahman, and when he thought of it as active he called it Śakti. They are not two. We cannot think of one without the other, as with fire and its power to burn, milk and its whiteness, or a gem and its lustre. Kashmir Śaivism says the same of Śiva and Śakti. See slide 5.

QUESTION 3

In the Devī Sūkta the seer Vāk says: “I am the sovereign Queen, gatherer of treasures, the Knower.” Why is this not arrogance?

A. She is a queen of the Vedic age and she is simply describing her own rank and her own great achievements.
B. She is praising the Goddess in poetic exaggeration, but she herself stays entirely separate from Her.
C. She is claiming a rare personal greatness, which the tradition permits to seers but not to ordinary devotees.
D. Having realised her oneness with the Goddess, she speaks as the Goddess Herself and not as a limited individual.✓ Correct answer

The Devī Sūkta (Ṛg Veda 10.125) is spoken by the seer Vāk, daughter of the sage Ambhṛṇa. Having realised her oneness with the Goddess, she speaks as the Goddess Herself: the power within every god, who rules, gives and knows, and whose breath is the life of the worlds. The voice is the Mother’s. The ego has not claimed it. See slide 6.

QUESTION 4

A speaker says in a talk: “Worship of God as Mother is a late folk addition, not part of the old Vedic religion.” What from the satsang would you offer in reply?

A. The Devī Sūkta in the Ṛg Veda (10.125) already speaks of the Goddess as the power within all the gods.✓ Correct answer
B. The Chaṇḍī was recited in Vedic times, and it is the oldest of the scriptures that speak of the Mother.
C. Sri Ramakrishna’s vision at Dakshineswar, which proves that the worship of the Mother has always been practised.
D. The Kashmir Śaiva texts, which are the oldest sources that speak of the Goddess as a conscious Power.

God as Mother is among the oldest scriptural voices of the Indian spiritual tradition. In the Devī Sūkta She is not one deity among many but the power within all the gods: the knower, the giver and the breath of the worlds. The Chaṇḍī (Devī Māhātmya) belongs to the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa and is traditionally recited together with this hymn. Kashmir Śaivism and Sri Ramakrishna’s life belong to later ages. See slide 6.

QUESTION 5

A listener says: “Physics says matter is energy, so science has now proved the Mother.” How does the satsang frame the relation between the two?

A. There is no connection at all, since physics studies matter and Vedanta deals only with the mind and its liberation.
B. It is a proof, because physics has shown that the world is one energy and that energy is what the seers meant by Śakti.
C. It is a proof for matter only, and the mind and the senses would need a separate science to be explained.
D. It is an analogy and not a proof; what Vedanta adds is that this one energy is conscious: Śakti, the Mother.✓ Correct answer

The notes say plainly: an analogy, not a proof. Physics describes matter as concentrated energy, and the seers saw every form as Śakti condensed into name and form. Swami Vivekananda taught that all matter comes from ākāśa and all force from prāṇa, both from one cosmic source. What Vedanta adds is that the one energy is not blind. It is conscious, and the tradition calls Her Mother. See slide 7.

QUESTION 6 · MATCHING

In the Chaṇḍī the demons stand for tendencies of the mind, and each is overcome by a different aspect of the Mother. Whose grace does each person most need?

1. Ben cannot get out of bed to practise, and lets weeks of dullness and spiritual sleep go by.✓ Mahākālī (wakes us from inertia)
2. Asha is restless, ambitious and quick to anger, and keeps finding new reasons to chase more.✓ Mahālakṣmī (gives strength over restless desire and ego)
3. Raj has made good progress and is now quietly proud: “I am the one who understands, and this is my own.”✓ Mahāsarasvatī (gives wisdom that dissolves subtle pride)
4. Kiran flares up whenever he is contradicted, and then regrets it for days.✓ Mahālakṣmī (gives strength over restless desire and ego)

A common inner reading sees the demons as tendencies of the mind that match the three guṇas. Madhu and Kaiṭabha, born while Viṣṇu sleeps, are inertia and spiritual sleep (tamas), and Mahākālī wakes us. Mahiṣāsura keeps changing shape like restless desire, anger and ego (rajas), and Mahālakṣmī gives the strength to conquer ourselves. Śumbha and Niśumbha are subtle pride, the sense of “I” and “mine”, and Mahāsarasvatī gives the wisdom that dissolves duality. See slide 8.

QUESTION 7

For years Daniel has tried to defeat his anger by willpower alone, and keeps losing. What does the inner reading of the Chaṇḍī suggest?

A. He should give up effort completely, since the Mother will fight the battle only for a person who does nothing.
B. He should keep trying harder, since the story shows that a determined effort always defeats the demon.
C. Effort matters, but the battle cannot be won by effort alone: we call on the Mother, and She fights it for us.✓ Correct answer
D. He should look for outer enemies, since the demons in the Chaṇḍī stand for people who threaten us.

The battle of the Chaṇḍī is fought within us, and the Mother fights it for us. We cannot win it by effort alone, so we call on Her, and She fights. The teaching joins effort and grace. We still make the effort, and it prepares us, while the victory is Hers. This is also the message of the image of the sail: the breeze of grace is always blowing and we need only unfurl the sail. See slide 8.

QUESTION 8

A student worries: “If the world is created, where did the material come from, and where is the stage on which it stands?” What do sūtras 1 and 2 of the Pratyabhijñāhṛdayam say?

A. The material was provided by Consciousness and the stage by Matter, so there are two principles at work in creation.
B. The question cannot be asked at all, since the sūtras teach that the universe is nothing but an illusion.
C. No outside material and no outside stage: She unfolds the universe on Her own screen, like a dream in the dreamer.✓ Correct answer
D. The universe stands on a stage prepared by earlier cycles of creation, which She merely rearranges again and again.

Citi, free Consciousness, is the one cause of the universe and needs nothing outside Herself. In a dream, the dreamer’s own mind is the stage, the actors and the scenery together. In the same way She is both the painter and the canvas. This echoes Sri Ramakrishna: Brahman and Śakti are one, like fire and its power to burn. See slide 9.

QUESTION 9

A meditator is exasperated with his restless mind and calls it his enemy, which he wants to defeat. How does the Kashmir Śaiva teaching (sūtra 5) reframe this?

A. The mind is already the same as the Self, so nothing needs to be done and no practice is required of anyone.
B. The mind is Citi Herself in a narrowed form, not an enemy; sādhanā is the return, in which it widens back into Her.✓ Correct answer
C. The mind is a separate and lower thing, which must be silenced completely before the Self can ever be known.
D. The mind is an illusion, so a person should simply ignore it and concentrate only on the body and its health.

Sūtra 5 completes the descent: Citi Herself, narrowing to what She perceives, becomes citta, the mind. Our mind is the Mother in disguise. Even restlessness is Her energy, waiting to be turned. So the mind is not an enemy, and sādhanā expands it back into Her. This is the return journey that the five sheaths describe in stages. See slide 10.

QUESTION 10

Someone says: “I feel so small, limited and separate. That is simply who I am.” How does sūtra 4 answer?

A. The feeling is contraction, not our nature: each of us is Consciousness contracted, like a bubble holding the ocean.✓ Correct answer
B. The feeling is a punishment for past errors and must be borne patiently until the next life brings some relief.
C. The feeling is a sign of weak faith, and a firmer belief in one’s own greatness and strength will remove it for good.
D. The feeling is correct, since only the Mother is great and every individual is truly small and limited beside Her vastness.

Sūtra 4 says that even the individual, being Consciousness contracted, holds the universe in contracted form. Each of us is the whole of Her, compressed, as a seed holds the tree. Feeling small and separate is the contraction. It is not the nature of the Self. The reflection question asks it directly: if I am a bubble in Her ocean, what do I have to fear? See slide 10.

QUESTION 11 · MATCHING

Sri Ramakrishna taught that Māyā has two faces. Which power is at work in each of these moments?

1. After a talk by a holy person, her mind turns gently inward and she feels a longing for God.✓ Vidyā Māyā (the power that frees)
2. A man cannot stop thinking “my reputation, my project, my team”, and feels cut off from others.✓ Avidyā Māyā (the power that binds)
3. A volunteer feels a wave of compassion for a stranger and quietly offers help.✓ Vidyā Māyā (the power that frees)
4. Someone chases a bigger house and each new purchase soon feels as empty as the last.✓ Avidyā Māyā (the power that binds)

Avidyā Māyā deludes: it pulls the mind outward through attachment to sense pleasures and possessions, the sense of “I and mine”, and forgetting who we are. Vidyā Māyā liberates: discrimination, devotion, compassion, longing for God and the company of the holy draw us inward. Both are Her powers, and the Upaniṣad says the mind alone is the cause of bondage and liberation, so the same mind can move either way. See slide 11.

QUESTION 12

Some people say: “If the goal lies beyond all Māyā, then devotion, discrimination and service are also Māyā, and I can drop them.” How does Sri Ramakrishna’s image of the steps to the roof answer?

A. The steps are the goal itself, so a person should stay on them and never think about reaching the roof at all.
B. He would agree that the steps may be dropped, since a person who knows the goal can jump to the roof directly.
C. We need the steps to reach the roof: we take refuge in Vidyā Māyā to climb, and the Mother then takes us beyond.✓ Correct answer
D. Both kinds of Māyā are equally good for climbing, and a person can choose the one that is more convenient.

Vidyā Māyā is like a ladder that carries us up to the roof, while Avidyā Māyā is like a mist that hides the sun without harming it. We take refuge in Vidyā Māyā, through discrimination, devotion and service, and the Mother Herself then takes us beyond both. The same Mother who binds is the Mother who sets free. See slide 11.

QUESTION 13

Two people see the same sunset. One is lost in craving to possess it. The other is quietened and turned inward. According to the Pratyabhijñāhṛdayam (sūtras 12 and 13), what accounts for the difference?

A. The second person has simply learned to stop seeing the sunset, so that the senses are shut down altogether.
B. Whether the seeing is recognised as Her power: when She turns it inward, the mind rises back towards Consciousness.✓ Correct answer
C. The two are using different sets of powers, which the first person lacks until he has completed his practice.
D. The first person is deluded by the world, which is unreal, while the second person has overcome the world.

The same four powers bind or free, depending on which way She turns them. Turned outward, in Her līlā, they hide Her nature and the jīva is deluded by its own powers: this is saṃsāra. Turned inward by grace, the mind (citta) rises back to Consciousness (citi). Recognising Her is the key. The practice is to recall, in any moment of seeing or thinking, “this too is Her power”. See slide 12.

QUESTION 14

A colleague’s sharp remark has upset you. The satsang’s practice is to pause and think, “this too is Her energy”. Which description best fits what this practice does?

A. It shows that the colleague was entirely right, so the anger can be dropped without any further thought.
B. It lets the feeling run its course, since it is Her energy and ought not to be interfered with at all.
C. It neither suppresses nor acts out the feeling: it recognises the energy as Hers, so it can turn inward.✓ Correct answer
D. It pushes the feeling away by an act of will, so that the mind returns to calm as quickly as possible.

In any moment of seeing or thinking, we can recall that this too is Her power. The energy of irritation is the same energy that, turned inward by Her grace, becomes devotion or clarity. Recognising the energy as Hers loosens its hold on us, neither crushing the feeling nor feeding it. The mind rises towards Consciousness, which is the practical meaning of the four powers turned inward. See slide 12.

QUESTION 15

A devotee says: “My craving and my confusion are the opposite of the Divine, so I must hide them from the Mother.” What does the Yā Devī hymn suggest?

A. The hymn teaches that these states are punishments, sent by the Mother to those who have failed Her in their devotion.
B. The hymn salutes only the good qualities, such as peace and compassion, and the others are not Hers at all.
C. The hymn salutes the Devī even as longing and confusion: nothing is outside Her, and even these are Her energy.✓ Correct answer
D. The hymn warns that craving and confusion are the work of a different force that stands against the Mother and must be fought.

The Chaṇḍī hymn salutes the Devī who dwells in all beings as consciousness, intelligence, sleep, hunger, power, patience, peace, faith, memory, compassion and mother, and even as delusion. Every faculty we use is Her power on loan to us. Even confusion is Her play, and nothing is outside the Mother. This does not excuse our faults. It tells us there is nothing we need hide from Her. QUESTION 16 · MATCHING

The five sheaths, read from the Mother’s side, are Her step-by-step contraction. Which sheath is at work in each of these moments?

1. You keep replaying an old insult and cannot stop imagining what you should have said.✓ Manomaya (mind and emotions)
2. You decide which task to do first, and feel “I decided this” and “this is mine”.✓ Vijñānamaya (intellect and ego)
3. In the night you wake and notice that you are being breathed, without any effort of yours.✓ Prāṇamaya (breath and vitality)
4. You eat, and the food becomes you, while a heart that you never chose keeps beating.✓ Annamaya (the body)

Manomaya is Her movement as feeling, memory and imagination. Vijñānamaya is discernment (buddhi) and the sense of “I” (ahaṁkāra), which can bind or be offered back. Prāṇamaya is the breath that breathes us, waking and sleeping. Annamaya is Her most contracted form, the food that becomes us and the heartbeat we never chose. Beyond and within all five is Parāśakti: we are bubbles in Her ocean. See slide 16.

QUESTION 17

A friend plans an expensive retreat that promises to “give” him spiritual power. What does the teaching on Kuṇḍalinī say about where that power comes from?

A. The power sits in the physical organs known as the chakras, and a particular exercise can start it moving.
B. The power belongs only to yogis, and ordinary people can only admire those who have already awakened it fully.
C. The power must be received from a special place or a special teacher who holds it in trust for the rest of us.
D. Spiritual life is not getting something from outside: it is waking the Mother’s power already resting within us.✓ Correct answer

Yoga describes Kuṇḍalinī as Śakti resting coiled at the base of the spine, our latent spiritual potential. Devotion, japa, longing and grace awaken Her, and as She rises through the centres, awareness rises and widens. Spiritual life is not getting something from outside. It is waking the Mother within. QUESTION 18

A beginner reads about Kuṇḍalinī and plans to practise forceful breathing alone late at night. What does the satsang advise?

A. She awakens safely through purity, devotion and guidance; for most of us the path is japa, prayer and meditation.✓ Correct answer
B. He should put off all practice until he has read every book on the subject and understood it fully, step by step.
C. He may begin alone, provided that he practises only for a few minutes each night and builds up very slowly.
D. He may go ahead, since the more forceful the practice, the faster the awakening comes to the practitioner.

Sri Ramakrishna described the rising current in vivid images: moving like an ant, a frog, a snake, a bird or a monkey. The awakening comes safely through purity, devotion and guidance. For most people the path is simple: japa, prayer and meditation on the Mother in the heart. Force and haste are not the way, and the Mother’s grace does the real work. See slide 17.

QUESTION 19

A devotee who has meditated for ten years without any vision says: “I must lack the right technique.” What does the life of Sri Ramakrishna suggest he might ask instead?

A. Whether there is intense longing, as of a child for its mother: Sri Ramakrishna wept each evening for Her.✓ Correct answer
B. Whether he has earned enough merit through years of discipline, since merit is what brings the Mother to a devotee.
C. Whether he is following the exact order of the steps in his practice, since that is what led to the first vision.
D. Whether he is serving as a priest in a temple, since the first vision came to Sri Ramakrishna in that very role.

Before his first vision, Sri Ramakrishna, then a priest at the Kālī temple, wept each evening that another day had passed without seeing Her. He spoke to Mā as a child speaks to its mother, simply and trustingly. The lesson is longing: if we cry for Her as a child cries for its mother, She cannot stay away. The satsang does not promise a vision on demand. It points to the heart of the practice. See slide 18.

These five paths suit different temperaments. Japa and meditation can be practised anywhere, and stotras and bhajans turn feeling towards Her. Sri Ramakrishna and Holy Mother both stressed regular practice done with love. See slide 22.

QUESTION 25

“The same Mother who binds is the Mother who sets free.” Which attitude to daily life follows from this?

A. Rely on our own effort alone, since the Mother is said to help only those who have first helped themselves.
B. Do nothing and wait, since everything depends on Her grace alone, whatever we may do or fail to do.
C. Run away from the world as quickly as possible, since it is the source of all our binding and our troubles.
D. Neither fight the world nor cling to it: take refuge in Her, use what turns us inward, and trust Her grace.✓ Correct answer

Avidyā Māyā and Vidyā Māyā are both Her powers, and the four powers within us bind or free depending on which way they are turned. So we neither fight the world nor cling to it. We take refuge in Vidyā Māyā, use whatever turns us inward, and trust that when She is gracious She turns our energies inward and reveals Herself. Effort and grace work together. See slides 11 and 12.

Reflect: Where in my life am I fighting, and where am I clinging? What would taking refuge in Her look like in that situation?
